How to Foster Inclusivity in Fitness Coaching: A Practical Guide for Coaches and Gyms

An inclusive coaching practice is not a separate program or a slogan on the wall. It is a set of ordinary decisions — about language, programming, space, pricing and feedback — that decide who feels they belong in the room. This guide covers the decisions coaches and gym owners can change this week.

↗

Start with the person, not the label

Disability, age, body size, chronic illness and previous injury all shape how someone moves — but none of them tells a coach what that person wants from training. Two people with the same diagnosis can have entirely different goals, preferences and support needs. The useful first step is a short conversation, not an assumption: what activities do you enjoy, what has worked before, what adaptations would help, and what should I know about how your body responds to effort?

Ask these questions of everyone, not only of people who look as though they might need adaptations. A standard intake that covers goals, health conditions, access needs and communication preferences normalizes the conversation and gives the coach information that improves programming for the whole group. Our companion guide to inclusive fitness looks at the same question from the participant's side: how to adapt movement and environment to different abilities and access needs.

Use language that invites people in

Coaching language is part of the environment. A few habits make a measurable difference:

  • Describe, don't judge. Say "lift the knee toward your chest" rather than "do it properly" or "the right way". Descriptive cues work for every body in the room.
  • Drop the hierarchy words. Avoid "normal", "real" or "ideal" when talking about bodies, and avoid "just" and "simply" when describing movements that may be difficult for some people.
  • Offer, don't order. "Try the seated version, or stay standing — both work" gives people a real choice. A single prescribed version, with an adaptation announced as an exception, singles people out.
  • Ask how people describe themselves. Person-first language is a good default, but preferences differ. A brief question at intake avoids repeated mistakes.

Language also covers what is on the walls and the website: photos and testimonials that show a range of ages, body sizes and abilities tell prospective members more than any mission statement.

Design sessions with options built in

The most practical inclusivity technique in coaching is to build options into the session plan itself. Instead of one movement plus an adaptation for "anyone who can't do it", plan two or three versions of every key movement from the start: a standing and a seated version, a higher- and lower-impact version, a version with support and one without. When options are part of the plan, choosing one is unremarkable.

Apply the same thinking to intensity and rest. A session that allows people to choose their effort level — and to take rest breaks without apology — works for a beginner, a returning member and an experienced athlete at the same time. Choosing cardio you enjoy and strength training for beginners both use this approach: the same session, scaled to the person doing it.

For group classes, announce options to the whole room and rotate demonstrations among different bodies when possible. If only the coach ever demonstrates, and the coach is always the fittest person in the room, the visual message is that the class belongs to people who already look like the coach.

Make the space itself welcoming

People decide whether to return within minutes of arriving. Coaches who control a space should walk it as a new member would: from the entrance to the changing area to the training floor.

  • Routes and surfaces: clear, step-free routes; non-slip floors; enough space between equipment for a wheelchair, a walking frame or a support person to pass.
  • Changing and toilets: accessible changing space, a private area for anyone who needs it, and clear signs rather than improvised directions.
  • Light and sound: very bright lights, loud music and strong fragrances can exclude people with sensory sensitivities, migraines or respiratory conditions. A quiet corner and a lower-volume hour cost little and widen the audience.
  • Information in more than one format: spoken instructions backed by a written or pictured version help people who process information differently, including anyone new to the language.

Adapt equipment and programming

Most gyms already own what they need. The gaps are usually selection and layout rather than budget: adjustable benches, resistance bands in several strengths, lighter dumbbells, a cable machine with a low anchor point, and a stable chair or box for seated work. Arrange equipment so that someone using a seated position can reach it without asking for help, and keep frequently used items at more than one height.

Programming adaptations follow the same principle as session design. Progress can mean more range of motion, better control, longer duration or a heavier load — not only more weight. A coach who tracks several measures of progress can celebrate improvement for every participant, including people whose progress will never look like a personal best on a barbell. Rethink pricing, policies and scheduling

Cost and commitment are access issues too. Long contracts, joining fees and peak-only class times quietly filter out people on low incomes, shift workers, carers and anyone whose health fluctuates. Practical changes include pay-per-session or short-block options, off-peak pricing, a free or low-cost trial that is genuinely usable, and a policy that welcomes a support person or carer at no extra charge.

Scheduling matters as much as price. A single weekly "accessible" class can help, but it also segregates. Better: make the regular timetable accessible and add quieter sessions at different times of day, so people can choose the environment that suits them rather than the one slot labelled for them.

Ask for feedback — and act on it

The fastest way to find barriers is to ask the people who meet them. Offer more than one way to give feedback — a short anonymous form, a quick chat after class, a suggestion box — and ask specifically: what made today harder than it needed to be? Then report back on what changed. A single visible fix (a ramp cleared, a class time moved, a cue reworded) builds more trust than a survey that disappears into a drawer.

Where possible, involve disabled people, older adults and people new to exercise in planning, not just as feedback-givers after the fact. Pay them for their time when the work is substantial. Lived experience is expertise, and it prevents the common pattern of designing for people without them.

What to do when you get it wrong

Every coach will get something wrong: a careless phrase, a session that excludes someone, an assumption made out loud. The repair is simple and worth practising: acknowledge it briefly, apologize without making it about your own feelings, fix what can be fixed, and change the practice that caused it. Most people are generous with a coach who listens and adjusts; what erodes trust is being corrected and carrying on as before.

Keep learning in small, regular doses rather than one annual training day. A short article, a conversation with a participant, a visit to another gym — each one adds a concrete change to try next week. Inclusivity in coaching is not a certificate to earn once; it is a habit of noticing who is missing and asking why.

Frequently asked questions

What does inclusivity mean in fitness coaching?

Inclusivity in fitness coaching means adapting language, programming, space and policies so people of different bodies, abilities, ages, backgrounds and budgets can take part safely and with dignity. It is a coaching practice, not a separate program.

How can a coach make a class inclusive without singling anyone out?

Offer options to the whole group instead of to one person: regressions and progressions for every movement, seated and standing versions, and choices of intensity. Use neutral cues and demonstrate more than one body type.

What equipment helps make a gym more accessible?

Adjustable benches, resistance bands, lighter dumbbells, seated exercise options, clear floor space and accessible changing facilities help. The most useful change is often layout: room to move, reach and transfer safely.

How should coaches talk about bodies and ability?

Use person-first, descriptive language, avoid words like normal, real or ideal to describe bodies, and ask people how they describe themselves. Focus coaching cues on what bodies can do rather than how they look.

A thoughtful next step

Pick one change from this guide and try it in your next session — one reworded cue, one built-in option, one walk through the space as a new member. Small changes, repeated, are what turn a welcoming intention into a gym where people actually stay. Consider your own circumstances, look for reliable sources, and seek qualified professional advice when a decision affects someone's health or safety.
